## The question, and why it is not "longer is better"

R-P1.57's two rivals — an **additive** disturbance and a **sign-flipped** reuse of
the same one — are distinguishable on matched inputs. That was measured at full
episode length. Two things could happen as the horizon shrinks:

- the two candidates' trajectories **diverge**, so a short horizon is enough;
- the trajectories **converge** again — both hit a wall, both settle, both end up
  in the same blind region — so a longer horizon adds nothing and a short one may
  be *more* discriminating.

Which one holds is the card's question, and it is answerable exactly.

## Design

R-P1.57's fixture (one pair, the same disturbance applied additively vs
sign-flipped), no noise, the lane's `motion_distance` over an observable
signature. Horizons `T ∈ {10, 20, 40, 60, 80, 120, 160, 200}` steps, 5 seeds.

For each horizon:

- **separation(T)** — `motion_distance` between the two candidates' observable
  signatures truncated at `T`, i.e. how telling the horizon is;
- **decidability(T)** — whether attribution at that horizon names the true rival
  uniquely (the verdict, not the distance);
- **control(T)** — the same two candidates scored against a **third**, unrelated
  episode, which must stay clearly separated so a shrinking distance is read as
  convergence rather than as the metric decaying.

## Frozen predictions

- **P1 — the anchor holds.** At `T = 200` (full episode) attribution names the true
  rival uniquely on 5/5 seeds, reproducing R-P1.57's direction.
- **P2 — separation is not monotone.** It **rises** from `T = 10` to some peak and
  then **falls or flattens** by `T = 200`, because both candidates reach the same
  boundary behaviour. Predicted: `separation(200) ≤ separation(peak)`.
- **P3 — a short horizon suffices.** The smallest `T` at which decidability is
  5/5 is **≤ 60** steps, i.e. well below the episode length.
- **P4 — the control stays separated at every T.** An unrelated episode's distance
  is larger than the rival distance at every horizon, so P2's fall is convergence
  of the rivals and not the metric losing power.
- **P5 — decidability is monotone in horizon up to the peak.** No horizon shorter
  than the sufficient one decides **more** than a longer one; a non-monotone
  decidability would mean the pairing is fragile in a way worth its own card.

Any prediction may fail and is kept. **P2 is the load-bearing one**: if separation
is monotone increasing, then the honest recommendation is "score as far as the
budget allows" and D3's horizon clause has a simple answer. If it falls, a
counterfactual has a **shelf life** and every card scoring at full length has been
using more evidence than it needed or than it should trust.

## What this card may and may not conclude

- It **may** say how far a counterfactual must be observed on this fixture, and
  whether more horizon stops helping.
- It **may not** claim a general shelf life: one geometry, one disturbance family,
  one metric. A different pair could converge at a different `T`.
- It **may not** retune R-P1.57, whose result stands at full length.
- It **may not** touch D3's contact/fold clauses — those need a hidden-contact
  fixture and are separate; this card closes only the horizon clause and says so.
- `E6` stays closed.

## Controls and honesty

- The unrelated-episode control is the one that separates "the rivals converged"
  from "the metric decayed"; without it P2 is unfalsifiable.
- Signatures are truncated, never resampled, so a horizon shorter than the episode
  is literally less evidence rather than a different measurement.
- Decidability is reported as a count over 5 seeds, not as a mean over distances.
- Verdict vocabulary: `PASS` / `FAIL` / `UNVERIFIABLE`.

## Limits

One pair, one disturbance family, eight horizons, five seeds, no noise. A
separately-noisy instrument would change when decidability is reached and is not
measured here.
