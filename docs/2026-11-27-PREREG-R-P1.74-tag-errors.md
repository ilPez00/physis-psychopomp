R-P1.46 measured producer **dependence** with **perfect content** — "perfect marker
content when visible, no noise or interventions" — and its tag control replaced
tags with one anonymous value. Neither tests what happens when a producer
delivers a **wrong tag**: the two delivered tags agree, but one of them is the
other world's.

That is the case a fusion rule has to survive, and it is the one a majority vote
gets wrong.

## Design, on R-P1.46's exact fixture

Two worlds differing only in a marker's **tag**; delivery masks `00/11` (shared),
all four (independent), `10/01` (complementary). R-P1.46's measured results are
the anchor: 1/2, 3/4, 1.

Added here, holding delivery marginals fixed:

- **W1 — one producer wrong.** Producer A delivers the tag of the *other* world,
  producer B delivers its own. Marginal delivery is unchanged (each producer still
  delivers in half the mass). Predicted: the pair resolves **1** in every arm,
  because A's value is constant across worlds and carries no information — a
  systematic bias is not noise.
- **W2 — both producers wrong the same way.** Both deliver the other world's tag.
  Predicted: resolves **1**, and the *decided* world is inverted. A fusion rule
  that only reports "they agree" would be confidently wrong, which is the point.
- **W3 — one producer wrong at random.** A's tag is independently wrong with
  probability 1/2 per delivery. Predicted: resolution **below** R-P1.46's perfect
  value but **above** chance, and the drop is larger in the independent arm than
  the shared one (more deliveries of A to corrupt).
- **W4 — matched anonymous control.** Strip all tags, as R-P1.46 did. Every arm
  must tie — the control that keeps the result from being a tag artefact.
- **W5 — matched tag-shuffled control.** Permute which producer carries the wrong
  tag across worlds. Predicted: resolution equals W1's, because a permutation does
  not change the information content, only which producer holds it.

Every arm keeps **delivery counts and sources identical**; only payload varies.
That is what "matched marginals" means here and the receipt records the counts to
prove it.

## Frozen predictions

- **P1 — the anchor holds.** With perfect content, 1/2, 3/4, 1 as R-P1.46 measured.
- **P2 — a systematically wrong producer is harmless.** W1 resolves 1 in all three
  arms: a constant offset is not evidence.
- **P3 — two wrong producers invert the decision.** W2 resolves 1 and picks the
  *wrong* world. Reporting only agreement is confidently wrong, and the receipt
  names which world each arm decides.
- **P4 — one wrong producer damages the independent arm more.** W3's resolution is
  strictly lower in the independent arm than in the shared arm, because more
  deliveries of A means more corrupted payloads.
- **P5 — the controls hold.** Anonymous ties in every arm (W4) and the tag shuffle
  is information-neutral (W5 equals W1).

Any prediction may fail and is kept. **P3 is the load-bearing one**: if two
consistently wrong producers still resolve to the right world, then agreement
counting is not the mechanism and R-P1.46's "fused distinguishability" needs
re-reading.

### Why: I predicted the wrong quantity

`resolves(...)` asks **"do the two worlds produce different delivered sequences"**,
and a constant tag error is a **relabelling**, not a loss of information. With both
producers wrong the delivered tags swap but still differ:

```
wrong = (False, False)   world0 delivers B1   world1 delivers A    differ
wrong = (True,  True)    world0 delivers A    world1 delivers B1   differ
```

So distinguishability is invariant under any constant per-producer tag offset —
which is a clean, checkable statement, and it is what the measurements show. My
predictions assumed the metric was "decides the right world", which it is not; the
card was re-reading its own fixture through the wrong question.

**The inversion is real and is the useful part:** W2 does make a
tag-matching rule name the *wrong* world (that clause held for the complementary
arm). So the honest summary is: a constant tag error **cannot be detected by
world-distinguishability at all**, and when a rule is asked to *name* the world it
will name the wrong one — confidently.

P4 failed for the same reason: a random error is still a per-delivery relabelling,
and with one marker per world there is nothing for it to degrade.

### What this closes, and what it does not

- **Closes:** D7's "incorrect tags at matched delivery marginals" question, with the
  answer that the *distinguishability* metric cannot see a constant tag error. Any
  future fusion rule must be scored on **decided-world accuracy**, not on whether
  the sequences differ, or it will report perfect discrimination on inverted data.
- **Matched marginals verified, not asserted:** delivery counts and sources are
  identical across all four payload rules for every mask (checked with an
  assertion in the receipt run), so the payload is the only variable.
- **Does not close:** correlated per-delivery errors, more than two producers, or a
  multi-marker world where a relabelling could break ties. The card's limits said
  the random model is a fair coin; that is now clearly the weaker half.

`E6` stays closed.
